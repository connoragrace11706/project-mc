# Why people share

The popular literature here is the weakest in the corpus and the platform
evidence is the strongest. Trust the platforms.

## What failed

**"High-arousal emotion causes sharing."** Berger's 2011 experiment failed to
replicate twice, at N=111 and N=160 [T3, Prowten et al. 2024]: "our studies cast
doubt on the idea that incidental physiological arousal impacts people's
decisions to share."

**STEPPS** has no peer-reviewed six-factor validation. It is a taxonomy, not a
model. Do not cite it as a mechanism.

## What survives

Berger & Milkman's 2012 field study [T3, 6,956 NYT articles] holds, and its
actual findings are not the ones people quote:

- **Practical value +30%** — matched awe, and **beat anxiety**.
- **Interest +25%** — also beat anxiety.
- All four emotion terms together added only ~.07 McFadden pseudo-R², rising to
  .28 only once length, homepage placement, author and section entered.

**Emotion is a minor term in a model dominated by placement and topic.** The
practical implication for a car/moto creator is direct: *useful* outperforms
*emotional*, and the corpus of top-performing automotive Shorts is overwhelmingly
instructional. This is not a coincidence.

## Sharing is a social act about the sharer

The platform evidence for this reading is much stronger than the psychology:

- Instagram runs **two separate prediction heads** for reshare-in-app and share
  off-platform [T1].
- Instagram's Ranking Explained lists reshare **first** among predictions [T1].
- Mosseri names sends-per-reach as the signal that unlocks non-follower reach
  [T2], and describes Instagram as a messaging app: "The primary way people
  share now is in DMs... That feed is dead."
- Meta reported Reels reshared over 4.5 billion times a day [T2].

Platforms model sharing as a **distinct behaviour**, not a byproduct of
watching. Something can be watched to completion and never sent.

## The send test

> **"This is for the person who ___."**

If you cannot finish that sentence with a real relationship, the piece will not
be sent. Not "is this good?" — *who is the named recipient?*

Automotive is unusually rich here because ownership is an identity:

- "for the person who has this exact car and doesn't know this yet"
- "for the friend who keeps saying he'll fix it himself"
- "for whoever told you this was a cheap car to run"
- "for the guy in your group chat who just bought one"

**Mandatory for Reels. Strong for TikTok. Ignorable for Shorts**, which is a
lean-back velocity feed where sends are not a named ranking input.

An explicit "send this to ___" works only when it names a **real relationship.**
"Tag a friend" is the marketing tell the audience is scanning for.

## Authenticity is not roughness

Direct contrary evidence exists: identical news stories at higher production
value were judged **more** credible [T3, Cummins & Chambers]. No controlled study
shows overproduced content gets less organic engagement.

What is penalised is **perceived commercial intent and genericness**, which is a
different thing entirely.

**Supported levers:**
- Personal disclosure [T3, Collins & Miller meta-analysis, d = .281 — modest but
  robust]
- Genuine subject-matter fit
- Explicit disclosure of commercial relationships

**Not supported — do not add these to sound human:**
- First-person pronouns
- Deliberate disfluency or unpolished syntax
- "Admitting uncertainty" as a generic reliability signal — hedges and
  hesitations move authority, sociability and character judgments in *different*
  directions [T3], so a hedge is not a free credibility gain

The automotive-specific version of the supported list: **hands in frame, your own
money, your own mistake.** Those are disclosure and subject-matter fit made
physical, and they are the cheapest credentials available in this niche.
