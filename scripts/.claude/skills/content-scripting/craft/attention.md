# Attention: how a hook actually works

The mechanism, not the templates. Templates are in `hooks.md`, and they are
downstream of this.

## The foothold requirement — the part everyone drops

**Attention is captured by a salient information gap opened inside a domain
where the viewer already has a foothold.** [T3, Loewenstein 1994]

Curiosity is *reference-point dependent*: "the same degree of knowledge can
evoke or not evoke curiosity depending on the level of one's reference point."
It **requires an existing knowledge base, not its absence.** Jones 1979 found
r = .51 between self-rated knowledge of an item and curiosity about it.

A gap opened in a domain the viewer has no purchase on produces **indifference,
not curiosity.** This is why every serious creator independently invented a
context-establishing beat *before* the hook — Kallaway calls it the "Context
Lean," MrBeast frames it as matching click expectations.

**Operationally:** before the gap, establish the domain. In automotive short-form
this is usually visual and costs no time at all — the part in frame, the engine
bay open, the bike in shot. The foothold beat and the gap beat can occupy the
same second. They cannot be skipped.

## The five triggers

Loewenstein's situational triggers are the operational core. Name which one a
hook uses; a hook that uses none is not a hook.

1. **A posed question or riddle.**
2. **A sequence with an anticipated but unknown resolution.** Amplified when the
   viewer generates their own prediction — this is the strongest of the five,
   and the prediction is what upgrades it.
3. **Violation of an expectation.** Also shifts the viewer from heuristic to
   systematic processing, meaning they start actually thinking about your
   content instead of scrolling past it.
4. **Someone else's possession of information.**
5. **Awareness of having once known something.**

Automotive translations: (2) is "will it start / what broke / how much did it
cost." (3) is the contrarian claim about a named object. (4) is the shop or
insider frame. (1) is the answerable physics question — *answerable* being the
operative word, since a question the viewer cannot reason about from the frame
is marketing, not a hook.

## Curiosity's default outcome is disappointment

Loewenstein again: satisfying a gap "eliminates the deprivation but leaves one
in a neutral state... the transition from aversive deprivation to a neutral
state is exceedingly fleeting," and he predicts satisfying cases "will be
outnumbered by those in which the information one receives is seen as
disappointing."

**This is the single most important design constraint in this corpus.** Every
gap you open incurs a debt, and the payoff must beat a baseline the gap itself
raised. See `curiosity-debt.md`.

## The concreteness band — most writers overshoot without knowing it exists

The Upworthy Registered Report [T3, 8,977 field experiments, 35,910 headlines]:

- Below 2.58 concreteness, adding specificity raised clicks ~**+5.5%**.
- Above 3.06, adding specificity **cut** clicks ~**−9.9%**.
- Only **8.7%** of real headlines benefited from more specificity.
- **50.9%** were actively hurt by it.

The lesson is not "be vague." It is that a narrow band exists, the failure is
asymmetric (overshooting costs nearly twice what undershooting gains), and most
writers have no awareness there is a band at all.

**A hook too specific to leave anything unresolved is as broken as one too vague
to signal a domain** — and the second failure is diagnosed far more often than
the first, which is why so much content is over-specified.

## What does not work

**Cliffhangers.** Raise arousal and **do not raise enjoyment or continuation
intent** [T3, Wirz et al., N=133, EDA + cortisol, replicated]. Spoilers do not
reliably reduce enjoyment.

**The Zeigarnik effect.** Does not replicate. 38 publications,
interrupted:completed recall ratio **0.99**, d_z ≈ 0.15 [T3, 2025
meta-analysis]. Never write "open loops work because the brain can't let go."

**What does replicate in the same literature** is the **Ovsiankina resumption
effect**: interrupted tasks are voluntarily resumed ~**67%** of the time across
21 publications. That is a behavioural return tendency, and it is a far better
model for "part 2" and series content than the memory claim ever was.

**Narrative transportation holds.** β = .236, 95% CI [.139, .332] [T3
meta-analysis]. Story works; the popular explanation for why it works does not.
