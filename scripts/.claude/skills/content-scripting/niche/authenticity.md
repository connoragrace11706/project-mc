# Authenticity rules

Hard constraints. **A single violation invalidates a whole script in the
comments.**

## Terminology traps

- Inline-four is **never** a V4.
- Turbos are exhaust-driven; superchargers are crank/belt-driven.
- WRX is the car; WRC is the series.
- **Bimmer = BMW car. Beemer = BMW motorcycle.**
- Porsche is two syllables.
- A 2JZ is not a 1JZ and neither is an RB.

**Getting the engine on screen wrong under the narration is the canonical
faceless death, and it is caught within minutes.**

## The catastrophising ceiling

Applied naively, a "front-load the jeopardy" rule generates *"your cabin air
filter is DESTROYING your engine."* **That register is the most-mocked voice in
the vertical.**

> **Jeopardy language is licensed only when the failure mode is real, named, and
> mechanism-explained.** If you cannot state the specific part, the specific
> failure, and the specific consequence, you may not use catastrophic verbs.

**Paired rule: no hyperbole about ordinary cars.** "This thing is an absolute
WEAPON" about a mainstream sedan trim reads as a dealership ad. Superlatives
require a superlative object.

## Provenance ladder — resolve before scripting

**Owned → borrowed from a friend → press fleet → rented → someone else's
entirely.**

This determines which archetypes are available (receipts and ownership
confession require Sam's own money) **and** triggers disclosure obligations
(`risk.md`). **Never imply ownership Sam does not have.**

## The faceless-credibility problem

The wrong rule, which a draft of the research contained: *"write faceless
scripts in first person with a stake — 'I bought this,' 'this happened to me.'"*
Over footage the narrator did not shoot, **that is an instruction to fabricate.**

> **Never write a sensory or first-person claim that the footage on screen
> cannot substantiate.** Write *around* footage you didn't shoot ("a stock 2JZ
> makes X — here's why the head gasket goes first"), never *inside* it. Credit
> third-party clips on screen. Where a claim needs presence, either get hands in
> frame or change the claim.

### Tells the audience catches, frame by frame

- Narration describes a sound the clip does not contain, or a music bed sits
  under "listen to that lifter tick."
- B-roll is the wrong generation, facelift, trim, or market — RHD footage under
  a US story, pre-facelift bumper under a facelift claim, wrong wheel, European
  plate.
- **The engine on screen isn't the engine in the script.**
- Mirrored or flipped stock footage, putting the shifter, exhaust, or timing
  side on the wrong side.
- Multiple angles of a discovery that is supposedly unfolding live.
- Any first-person claim with no hands, no shop, no continuity.

### Four faceless models that survive

All of them relocate authority away from ownership:

1. **Analysis over credited clips.** You own the read, not the footage, and you
   say so on screen.
2. **Documented / archival framing.** Auction results, recall filings, NHTSA
   complaint data, EPA figures, period press. Nobody expects presence.
3. **The object in your own hand.** Strictly hands-only — not faceless, and
   that's the point. The cheapest credential there is.
4. **Mechanism / physics claims.** Checkable, so no credential needed.

## Gear content — first person only

**You may not gear-shame a third party.** *"Gear nazi"* and *"ATGATT nanny"* are
live pejoratives and the counter-reaction is as strong as the norm. The
defensible position is first-person ("I wear it because I've slid") plus the
pragmatic line — **some gear beats none.**

Two exceptions that raise the bar sharply:

- **If a rider in your footage is visibly under-geared, address it in the script
  or don't use the footage.** Silence reads as endorsement and derails the
  comment section off your actual point.
- **Escalate hard when a passenger or a child is in frame.** Community tolerance
  for personal risk is broad; tolerance for *imposed* risk is near zero. This is
  precisely where creator standing collapses.

## Comment provocation, done natively

**The strongest verified finding here contradicts standard CTA advice.** Two
measured cases:

- An explicit CTA — "What would you rather buy and why? 👇" — drew 532 comments,
  but the **top comment (453 likes) ignored the binary entirely and nitpicked a
  model name.** The third (264 likes) named a bike never mentioned.
- "What's the mileage on the odometer??" drew 414 comments; the top comments
  **ignored the question and discussed a coworker's demeanour.**

> **A nitpickable detail and a character beat out-drive an explicit CTA
> question.**

### What to write instead

- **Plant one genuinely nitpickable detail** — a badge, a model designation, a
  trim distinction, a naming quirk — and let it sit.
- **Year/trim-range targeting** creates a legitimate correction surface: owners
  outside the range will say so.
- **Price and loyalty binaries asked affectionately**, not to farm outrage.
  "HOW MUCH DID YOU PAY?" ran 404K.
- **A specific ask tied to the content** — "what would you have done?" after a
  judgment clip, "what's the part you'd never buy used?" after a parts video.

**Generic "follow for more car content" is the marketing tell the audience is
scanning for.**

### Live arguments worth taking a side in

State a position, give the strongest opposing case, explain the tiebreak:
manual vs auto; EV driving character; loud pipes (**only** when attached to a
specific documented near-miss or a measured test); ATGATT vs personal choice;
stance vs function; restomod vs numbers-matching; overland gear vs actual trail
use; "is X a real enthusiast car."

### Bait that gets punished

- Rider-vs-driver rage bait where the rider creates the hazard and the edit
  blames the motorist.
- Deliberate mechanical errors planted to farm corrections.
- Staged shop "discoveries."
- Obvious-answer ID posts.
- **Exhausted brand jokes** — "BMW drivers never signal," Subaru head gaskets,
  "the Supra is a BMW," VTEC jokes. These produce jokes, not discussion, and
  carry no argument. *Caveat: worn out for a cold account; still available to a
  brand with a decade of equity behind it.*
